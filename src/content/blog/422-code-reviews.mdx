Should engineering managers stay in code review? Yes, but not to control. Review to learn. Between June and October 2026, I reviewed 422 pull requests while managing a team of 3 at Justworks. Here's what that volume teaches you that you can't get any other way, and what it costs.

The standard advice for new engineering managers is simple: stop coding. Your job is the team now: 1:1s, planning, hiring, unblocking. The code will survive without you.

I didn't take that advice. I'm a player-coach: I manage a team of 3, I own frontend for all things onboarding and billing, and I'm still in the code.

## The numbers

| Metric                        | Value       |
| ----------------------------- | ----------- |
| PRs reviewed (Jun-Oct 2026)   | 422         |
| Team PRs merged (same period) | 231         |
| Team size                     | 3 engineers |
| Reviews per workday (avg)     | ~5          |
| Diffs read per month          | 100+        |

## Standards stay calibrated to reality

Managers who stop reading code develop opinions about quality based on memory. Memory drifts. Reading 100+ diffs a month keeps your sense of "good enough" anchored to what the team actually ships, not what you shipped three years ago.

## You see struggles before the team tells you

When three different engineers hit the same confusing API, that's not three skill problems. It's a design problem. Review is the cheapest observability you have into your own systems. No dashboard tells you where the abstractions are leaking; the diffs do.

## Mentorship compounds in public

A thoughtful comment on a PR teaches once and is visible to the whole team. The same feedback in a 1:1 teaches once and disappears. Review comments are the highest-leverage teaching surface a manager has.

## The cost is real

Four hundred reviews in four months is roughly five a day, every workday. That's context-switching on top of management work, and some weeks it meant reviews happened early in the morning or not at all. I don't review everything. I review the risky things, the patterns, and the PRs from engineers who are still leveling up.

## The payoff is trust

When I make a technical call, the team trusts it, because they watched me earn it in their diffs, not in a meeting. You can't delegate credibility.

If you're a new manager wondering whether to keep reviewing: don't review to control. Review to learn. The moment it becomes about gatekeeping instead of understanding, stop.

## FAQ

**Should every engineering manager review code?**
No. It depends on team size, the manager's technical background, and where the team needs leverage. For a small team where the manager was recently an IC, review is high-leverage. For a team of 15, it's a bottleneck.

**How much time does 422 reviews actually take?**
Roughly five reviews per workday over four months. Most take 10-20 minutes; risky ones take longer. It's real time, and it competes with 1:1s, planning, and hiring.

**Doesn't reviewing everything make you a bottleneck?**
Yes, if you review everything. I don't. I review the risky changes, the patterns that repeat, and PRs from engineers who are still leveling up. The goal is learning, not approval.

**What if my team resents manager review?**
Then stop, or change how you do it. Review as a student, not a judge. Ask questions more than you give directives. The moment it feels like surveillance, it's hurting more than helping.

**Can AI do this instead?**
AI review catches style and obvious bugs. It doesn't calibrate your judgment, surface systemic design problems, or build trust with your team. Those are the reasons to review.
